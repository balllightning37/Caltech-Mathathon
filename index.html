1Core+: deviating by fractions

Briefly, from the previous tab: a coalition \(S\) blocks a committee \(W\) when it can afford some proposal \(T\) — meaning \(|S| \ge q\,|T|\) for the quota \(q\) — and every member of \(S\) strictly prefers \(T\) to \(W\). A committee is in the core when no coalition blocks it. That is demanding: the committee maximising PAV in the paper's Figure 2 instance is blocked, and verifying membership is coNP-complete. The question is whether a committee in the core must exist at all.

Here is the first real move. Instead of weakening the requirement to make it easier to satisfy, the paper strengthens it — and the stronger property turns out to be the tractable one.

A fractional blocking coalition is a participation vector \(y \in \mathbb{R}^n_{\ge 0}\setminus\{0\}\) and candidate quantities \(z \in \mathbb{R}^m_{\ge 0}\) which the coalition can afford, \(\sum_i y_i \ge q \sum_c z_c\), and under which every voter strictly gains:

\[ \sum_{c \in A_i \setminus W} z_c \;+\; \sum_{c \in A_i \cap W} \min\{y_i, z_c\} \;\ge\; \bigl(u_i(W)+1\bigr)\, y_i \quad\text{for every } i \in N. \tag{2} \]

A committee satisfies core+ if no such coalition exists. Setting \(y \in \{0,1\}^n\) and \(z \in \{0,1\}^m\) recovers condition (1) exactly, so core+ implies the core. Two relaxations have been layered on:

  • Allowing \(z_c \in [0,1]\) lets the coalition propose a fractional committee, where a voter draws utility \(z_c\) from an approved candidate \(c\).
  • Allowing \(y_i \in [0,1]\) lets a voter deviate partially. The \(y_i\) fraction of her goes with the coalition while the rest stays with \(W\) — which is why approved winners contribute only \(\min\{y_i, z_c\}\). The part of her that stayed behind is already enjoying \(c\); she can collect at most the remaining \(y_i\) from it.

All the inequalities are homogeneous, so any feasible deviation can be rescaled into the unit cube — try the sliders. The pay-off for this extra generality is structural: the set of blocking coalitions is now described by linear inequalities, which is what makes duality available.

2Payment systems as certificates

Testing core+ directly means checking that a linear program is infeasible. Dualise it, and infeasibility becomes the existence of an object you can hold in your hand. The paper's name for it is a payment system, and it is best read as an accounting story.

Give every voter a budget of exactly 1. She splits it between payments \(p_{ic}\) to the committee members she approves, and a reserve \(r_i\) she keeps back. (Voters never pay for candidates they do not approve.) Four conditions:

conditions (P1)–(P4)

(P1) \(\;r_i + \sum_{c \in A_i \cap W} p_{ic} = 1\) for every voter — a unit budget, fully allocated.

(P2) \(\;r_i \ge p_{ic}\) for every payment — no voter commits more to any single committee member than she keeps for outside options. Every payment is backed by an equally large reserve.

(P3) \(\;\sum_{i \in N_c} r_i < q\) for every loser \(c \notin W\) — the supporters of an unelected candidate do not jointly hold a quota in reserve. Otherwise they would have a justified complaint that \(c\) was left out.

(P4) \(\;\sum_{i \in N_c} p_{ic} \le q\) for every winner \(c \in W\) — no elected candidate collects more than a quota. Otherwise its supporters were overcharged.

Write \(\mathcal{P}_{1234}(W)\) for the set of payment systems meeting all four. The equivalence is exact:

Theorem 3.2

A committee \(W\) satisfies core+ if and only if \(\mathcal{P}_{1234}(W) \neq \emptyset\).

One direction is worth seeing by hand, because it shows what the conditions are for. Suppose \(W\) has a certificate but is blocked by \(S\) with proposal \(T\), so \(|S| \ge q|T|\). Every \(i \in S\) strictly improves, so each approved winner that \(T\) drops can be matched to a distinct approved candidate in \(T\setminus W\), with at least one left over. Now imagine each voter paying her usual \(p_{ic}\) to the candidates in \(T \cap W\), and her full reserve \(r_i\) to the new ones — legitimate, because by (P2) the reserve is at least as large as any payment she was making. Each voter in \(S\) therefore pays out at least her whole unit budget, so \(T\) collects at least \(|S|\). But counting per candidate instead: winners in \(T\) take at most \(q\) each by (P4), and losers at most \(\sum_{i\in N_c} r_i < q\) by (P3). So \(T\) collects strictly less than \(q|T|\). Together, \(|S| < q|T|\) — contradicting affordability.

So the two "fairness" conditions pull in opposite directions, and that tension is the whole problem. (P3) wants reserves high, so that no loser looks neglected. (P4) wants payments low, so no winner is overpaid. Since (P1) ties payments and reserve together, you cannot simply satisfy both. In the figure below, try to repair the (P4) violation on W: every fix raises someone's reserve until c3 or c4 breaks (P3). On W′ it can be done.

Conditions (P1)–(P3) alone already buy a lot: any committee admitting such a system satisfies EJR+, is \((\ell-1)\)-representative for every \(\ell\), and admits no disjoint core deviations (Proposition 3.3). The difficulty is entirely in adding (P4).

3What PAV gets right, and wrong

Proportional approval voting picks the committee maximising

\[ \mathrm{PAV}(W) \;=\; \sum_{i \in N} H_{u_i(W)}, \qquad H_t = \sum_{j=1}^{t} \frac1j, \quad H_0 = 0. \]

A voter's first approved winner is worth 1, the second \(1/2\), the third \(1/3\), and so on, so PAV is reluctant to pile utility on voters who already have plenty.

PAV secretly is a payment rule. Give each voter the uniform payment system: split her budget into \(u_i(W) + 1\) equal parts, one per approved winner plus the reserve, so

\[ r_i \;=\; p_{ic} \;=\; \frac{1}{u_i(W)+1}. \]

This satisfies (P1) and (P2) by construction. And now the key identity: a voter's reserve is \(1/(u_i+1)\), which is exactly what one more approved winner would add to her harmonic score. So

Theorem 4.1

(a) For every loser \(c\), \(\;\mathrm{PAV}(W \cup \{c\}) - \mathrm{PAV}(W) = \sum_{i \in N_c} r_i\).

(b) For every \(W'\) with \(|W'| = k+1\) there is some \(d \in W'\) with \(\;\mathrm{PAV}(W') - \mathrm{PAV}(W' \setminus \{d\}) \le \tfrac{n}{k+1}\).

Hence a PAV-optimal committee's uniform payment system satisfies (P1)–(P3).

The swap argument in that last line is the template for everything that follows. If some loser \(c\) violated (P3), gathering \(\sum_{i \in N_c} r_i \ge q\) in reserve, then by (a) adding \(c\) would raise PAV by at least \(q\). By (b) we could then delete some candidate for at most \(n/(k+1)\). Since \(q > n/(k+1)\), the net effect of this single swap is a strict improvement — contradicting optimality. Part (b) is pure averaging: the total of all \(k+1\) deletion costs is at most \(n\), so the cheapest is at most \(n/(k+1)\).

But PAV stops there, and the figure shows why. On \(W\), voters \(v_1\) and \(v_2\) each pay \(1/3\) to both \(c_1\) and \(c_2\), so each of those candidates collects \(2/3 > q = 1/2\). Condition (P4) fails: PAV overcharges \(v_1\) and \(v_2\). And indeed \(W\) is exactly the committee that the coalition \(\{v_1, v_2\}\) blocked on the previous tab.

Forcing (P4) to hold here would push \(r_1\) or \(r_2\) up to \(q\), breaking (P3) for \(c_3\) or \(c_4\). The uniform payment system is simply too rigid: it gives every coordinate the same value. Note also that PAV cannot even tell \(W\) apart from \(W' = \{c_1,c_2,c_3,c_5,\dots,c_9\}\) — the two have identical PAV scores, but \(W'\) does admit a full certificate. What is needed is an objective that sees the shape of a voter's payments, not just how many winners she approves.

4The Robin Hood operator

By (P1) and (P2), a voter's reserve and payments form a probability distribution in which the reserve is the largest coordinate. So we need a function on distributions that rewards balance, agrees with harmonic numbers at the uniform distribution, and — crucially — preserves the marginal-gain identity that made the PAV swap argument work.

The construction starts with an operation that adds coordinates to a distribution by levelling down the tall ones. For \(x \in \Delta_d\) and \(\ell \ge 1\), the Robin Hood operator is

\[ \rho_\ell(x) \;=\; \bigl(\min\{x_1,t\},\dots,\min\{x_d,t\},\underbrace{t,\dots,t}_{\ell}\bigr), \]

where the water level \(t\) is the unique solution of

\[ \ell \cdot t \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{d}(x_j - t)^{+}. \]

In words: pick the height \(t\) at which the mass skimmed off everything above \(t\) is exactly enough to fill \(\ell\) brand-new coordinates up to \(t\) as well. Total mass is preserved, and no existing coordinate ever increases.

Two facts make it the right operation. First, \(\rho\) preserves uniformity: \(\rho(\tfrac1d,\dots,\tfrac1d) = (\tfrac{1}{d+1},\dots,\tfrac{1}{d+1})\) — precisely what the uniform payment system does when a candidate is added. Second, repeatedly applying \(\rho\) to any strictly positive distribution reaches the uniform distribution in finitely many steps. The reason is neat: whenever \(\rho\) changes a coordinate it becomes a largest coordinate, and a largest coordinate stays largest; after enough steps the new coordinates are smaller than any original one, so every original must have been levelled at some point — and so all of them are maximal.

The water level also has a closed form that makes it computable, and which the figure uses (Lemma 5.1):

\[ t_\ell(x) \;=\; \max_{\emptyset \neq S \subseteq [d]} \frac{\sum_{j \in S} x_j}{|S| + \ell}. \tag{4} \]

5Harmonic entropy

Now define, for strictly positive \(x \in \Delta_d\),

\[ F(x) \;=\; \begin{cases} H_{d-1} & \text{if } x = (\tfrac1d,\dots,\tfrac1d),\\[4pt] F(\rho(x)) - x_{\max} & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases} \]

extended to distributions with zeros by deleting them. This is well defined exactly because \(\rho\) drives everything to uniform in finitely many steps — the recursion always bottoms out. Rearranged, the definition is the marginal-gain identity we were chasing:

\[ F(\rho(x)) \;=\; F(x) + x_{\max} \qquad\text{for all } x \in \Delta_d. \]

Introducing a new coordinate through \(\rho\) increases \(F\) by the largest coordinate of the original distribution — and by (P2) that largest coordinate is the voter's reserve. This is the non-uniform generalisation of Theorem 4.1(a), and it is the hinge of the whole proof.

\(F\) is also continuous, concave, symmetric, and invariant under adding zero coordinates (Proposition 5.2). Concavity plus symmetry means \(F\) is maximised at the uniform distribution, where it equals \(H_{d-1}\) — so harmonic entropy recovers the PAV score on uniform payments, and scores every other shape strictly lower. There is an equivalent series form,

\[ F(x) \;=\; \sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\Bigl(\frac{1}{\ell+1} - t_\ell(x)\Bigr), \tag{6} \]

which is what makes \(F\) differentiable enough to optimise numerically — the diagram below uses the recursion and the series side by side as a consistency check.

The name is earned. Replacing the sum over integer \(\ell\) in (6) with an integral over continuous added capacity turns \(F\) into Shannon entropy, \(-\sum_j x_j \ln x_j\), and \(H_{d-1}\) into \(\ln d\). Harmonic entropy is a discrete analogue of Shannon entropy — one whose marginal gains are exact reciprocals, which is precisely what PAV needs.

6The harmonic entropy rule

Everything is now in place. For a committee \(W\), optimise jointly over all payment systems that satisfy the easy conditions and the cap:

\[ V(W) \;=\; \max_{(r,p)\,\in\,\mathcal{P}_{124}(W)} \;\sum_{i \in N} F(r_i, p_i). \]

The maximum is attained: \(\mathcal{P}_{124}(W)\) is non-empty (take \(r_i = 1\) and no payments), compact and convex, and \(F\) is continuous. The harmonic entropy rule selects the committees maximising \(V\). Note what has happened to (P3): it is not a constraint here. It is the thing we are going to deduce.

Adding a candidate

If \(W\) has an optimal payment system under which some loser \(c\) hoards a quota of reserve, then electing \(c\) is worth at least a quota of objective:

Theorem 6.2

Let \((r,p) \in \mathcal{P}_{124}(W)\) be optimal. If some \(c \notin W\) has \(\sum_{i \in N_c} r_i \ge q\), then \(V(W \cup \{c\}) \ge V(W) + q\).

The proof applies \(\rho\) to each supporter's distribution, reading the new coordinate as her payment for \(c\). Conditions (P1) and (P2) survive — \(\rho\) outputs a distribution, and the reserve stays a largest coordinate — and (P4) survives for the old winners because \(\rho\) never raises a coordinate. By the marginal-gain identity, the objective rises by exactly \(\sum_{i \in N_c} r_i\). That might overshoot the cap on the new candidate \(c\), so one takes a convex combination with the original system, weighted by \(\lambda = q / \sum_{i \in N_c} r_i \in (0,1]\); concavity of \(F\) then delivers a gain of at least \(\lambda \cdot \sum_{i\in N_c} r_i = q\).

Deleting a candidate

Theorem 6.3

For any committee \(W\) with \(|W| \ge k+1\) there exists \(d \in W\) with \(\;V(W \setminus \{d\}) \ge V(W) - \tfrac{n}{|W|}\).

This is the harder half. The averaging trick needs payments that have been pushed as high as the cap allows, so the paper first de-reserves: starting from an optimal system, every voter with leftover reserve who approves an unsaturated winner pours that reserve into her payments, at unit rate, until either her reserve is exhausted or the candidate hits \(q\). What survives is a system in which any remaining reserve belongs to voters all of whose approved winners are saturated. Lemma 6.4 then shows this can be arranged so that, for an unsaturated \(d\), each supporter's payment to \(d\) is her largest, and

\[ F(\hat r_i, \hat p_i) \;=\; F(r_i, p_i) - \hat p_{id}. \]

Because total payments are at most \(n\), the candidate \(d\) with the smallest total receives at most \(n/|W|\), which is below \(q\) — so \(d\) is unsaturated, and deleting it costs at most that. The witness system for \(W \setminus \{d\}\) simply relabels each supporter's payment to \(d\) as her new reserve, which is legal precisely because of Lemma 6.4(a).

Putting the two together

Theorem 6.5

Let \(\varepsilon = q - \tfrac{n}{k+1} > 0\), and let \(W \in \mathcal{W}_k\) be such that \(V(W) > V(W \cup \{c\} \setminus \{d\}) - \varepsilon\) for all \(d \in W\) and \(c \notin W\). Then \(W\) satisfies core+. In particular every committee selected by the harmonic entropy rule satisfies core+.

Contrapositive. If \(W\) fails core+ then by Theorem 3.2 its optimal system violates (P3), so some loser \(c\) has \(\sum_{i\in N_c} r_i \ge q\). Theorem 6.2 gives \(V(W\cup\{c\}) \ge V(W) + q\); Theorem 6.3, applied to the now \((k+1)\)-sized committee, finds a \(d\) with \(V(W\cup\{c\}\setminus\{d\}) \ge V(W\cup\{c\}) - \tfrac{n}{k+1}\). Chaining them,

\[ V(W \cup \{c\} \setminus \{d\}) \;\ge\; V(W) + q - \frac{n}{k+1} \;=\; V(W) + \varepsilon. \]

So a single swap improves \(V\) by at least \(\varepsilon\) — and \(W\) was not even an approximate local optimum. Walk the numbers below on the very committee that failed the core in Section 3.

Because only local optimality is needed, this also yields the polynomial-time algorithm of Section 7: hill-climb on \(V\) by single-candidate swaps, and stop when no swap gains \(\varepsilon\). Finding a global maximiser is NP-hard (Proposition 6.9), but one never needs to.

Finally, here is the whole instance at once: all 45 committees of size 8, scored both ways. Maximising \(V\) lands in the core every time; maximising PAV does not.

7Why this proves existence

Read the chain backwards and the existence proof is three lines.

  1. The objective \(V\) is a real-valued function on the finite set \(\mathcal{W}_k\) of committees, so it attains a maximum. Nothing deep — just finiteness.
  2. By Theorem 6.5 any maximiser — indeed any committee no single swap improves by \(\varepsilon\) — satisfies core+.
  3. Core+ implies the core. Therefore the core is non-empty.
The shape of the argument

The core is a statement about what cannot happen, quantified over exponentially many coalitions, and it is coNP-complete to verify. Strengthening it to core+ makes it a statement about an LP being infeasible; duality turns that into the existence of a certificate; and harmonic entropy is engineered so that the certificate falls out of an optimality condition. The hard part was not the optimisation — it was finding the objective whose marginal gains are exactly a voter's unspent budget.

What the paper does after this point, and this page does not cover: Section 7 turns local search into a polynomial-time algorithm in the input size and \(1/\varepsilon\); Section 8 extends everything to the Droop quota, where \(\varepsilon \to 0\) and the above slack argument needs more care; and the appendices establish the properties of \(F\) used here, the equivalence in Theorem 3.2, and the relationship to Lindahl priceability, EJR+ and Pareto optimality.
