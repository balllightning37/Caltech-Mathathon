1The question that was open

A city has to pick 8 projects from a list of 10. Every resident marks the ones they like. Which 8 should win? Picking the most popular ones can leave a sizeable minority with nothing. What we want is a committee that is stable: no group of voters, large enough to deserve a share of the budget, can point to a different slate of proportional size that every one of them likes strictly better.

That stability notion is the core, introduced for this setting by Aziz, Brill and co-authors in 2017. The obvious question — does a committee in the core always exist? — stayed open for years. Algorithms were known that got within a constant factor, or that worked in special cases, but nobody could rule out an election in which every single committee is blocked by some coalition.

The result

The core is always non-empty. Moreover a committee in the core can be computed in polynomial time, and it satisfies a strictly stronger property the authors call core+.

The proof is not a direct construction, and it is not a fixed-point argument. It works by inventing a quantity to maximise. The route has four moves, which the next tab walks through in turn:

  1. Strengthen the core to core+, by letting coalitions deviate fractionally. A stronger property is harder to satisfy, but it has far better structure.
  2. Dualise. By LP duality, core+ is equivalent to the existence of a payment system — a certificate you can check by inspection.
  3. Score. Invent an objective, harmonic entropy, that rewards balanced payments and whose marginal gains line up exactly with a voter's unspent budget.
  4. Optimise. Show that any committee maximising that objective — even only locally — must admit a valid certificate. Existence follows, because a maximum exists.

2Elections, committees, quotas

There are \(n\) voters \(N = [n]\) and \(m\) candidates \(C = \{c_1,\dots,c_m\}\), and we must elect a committee of \(k\) of them. Voter \(i\) submits a set \(A_i \subseteq C\) of approved candidates, and her utility for a committee \(W\) is simply how many of her approvals got in:

\[ u_i(W) \;=\; |A_i \cap W|. \]

We write \(N_c = \{i \in N : c \in A_i\}\) for the supporters of candidate \(c\). One more quantity does a lot of work: the quota \(q\), the number of voters needed to deserve one candidate. The Hare quota is \(n/k\) and the Droop quota is \(n/(k+1)\); throughout, the paper assumes

\[ \frac{n}{k+1} \;<\; q \;\le\; \frac{n}{k}. \]

That strict left-hand inequality looks like bookkeeping. It is not — the gap \(\varepsilon = q - n/(k+1) > 0\) is exactly the slack that the final argument spends, and the whole proof turns on its being positive.

3The core, and how it fails

A non-empty coalition \(S \subseteq N\) blocks a committee \(W\) if it can afford some proposal \(T \subseteq C\) — meaning \(|S| \ge q\,|T|\), the coalition is large enough to pay for \(|T|\) candidates at \(q\) voters each — and every member strictly prefers it:

\[ u_i(T) \;\ge\; u_i(W) + 1 \quad\text{for every } i \in S. \tag{1} \]

\(W\) is in the core if no coalition blocks it. The definition is demanding in a subtle way: the coalition gets to choose both who joins and what to propose, and those choices interact. Deciding whether a given committee is in the core is already coNP-complete, which is a fair warning about the structure one is dealing with.

The figure below is the paper's Figure 2, and it is the example to keep in mind for the rest of this page. Voters v1 and v2 both approve c1 and c2, and each has one private candidate; v3 and v4 jointly approve a block of six. With \(k=8\) and the Hare quota \(q = 4/8 = 1/2\), the committee \(W = \{c_1,c_2,c_5,\dots,c_{10}\}\) looks reasonable and in fact maximises PAV — yet \(\{v_1,v_2\}\) can afford \(\{c_1,c_2,c_3,c_4\}\), since \(|S| = 2 = q\cdot 4\), and both of them go from utility 2 to utility 3.

4Where this goes next

So the core asks for a lot, and the committee above shows that the obvious proportional rule does not deliver it. Two questions remain, one per tab:

  • Why the core exists. Does a committee in the core always exist? The answer is yes, and the proof is indirect: strengthen the core to core+, dualise it into a payment certificate, and invent an objective whose maximisers must admit one.
  • Computing one. Can we actually find such a committee? Yes, in polynomial time — and you can watch the algorithm run, step by step, on an instance you build yourself.
